General Introduction

The digital transformation of university services has become a strategic priority for higher education institutions seeking to improve operational efficiency, optimize resource management, and enhance student experience. Among these services, university restaurants represent a critical component of campus life, as they must deliver affordable meals to a large population within limited time windows and under strict logistical constraints. Traditional manual management methods, such as physical queues, cash payments, and paper-based verification, are increasingly inadequate for handling modern demand patterns and ensuring service quality during peak periods.

At the Higher Institute of Technological Studies in Communications of Tunis (ISETCOM), the university restaurant serves several hundred students daily. Before the implementation of the proposed digital system, the service relied entirely on manual workflows that generated long waiting times, inefficient capacity utilization, and limited visibility into consumption trends. The absence of digital traceability made it difficult to perform accurate planning, evaluate service performance, or implement continuous improvement strategies. These limitations not only affected student satisfaction but also contributed to increased staff workload and unnecessary food waste.

Advances in mobile technologies, cloud computing, and data-driven analytics offer new opportunities to modernize campus services through scalable and secure digital platforms. Mobile applications enable real-time interaction with users, while cloud-based infrastructures support elasticity and centralized data management without the need for complex on-premise deployments. 
 
Within this context, the objective of this project is to design, develop, and deploy a mobile application that fully digitalizes the meal reservation, payment, and access validation workflow of the ISETCOM university restaurant. The solution integrates cross-platform mobile development using Flutter, cloud services provided by Firebase, secure national payment systems. Beyond technical implementation, the project aims to demonstrate that a student-led development team can deliver a production- grade solution that meets real institutional needs and operates reliably under daily operational constraints.

This report documents the complete engineering process, starting from institutional context analysis and stakeholder needs assessment, through requirements specification and system design, to implementation, performance evaluation, and impact assessment. Each chapter contributes to building a comprehensive understanding of how modern software engineering practices can be applied to solve real-world problems within the academic environment.

1.2	Project Presentation
The ISETCOM University Restaurant Mobile Application project represents a comprehensive digital transformation initiative aimed at modernizing meal service operations through mobile technology. The project addresses critical operational inefficiencies in the current manual system, including excessive waiting times during peak hours, limited payment options, and lack of real-time capacity management.

The proposed solution leverages Flutter framework for cross-platform mobile development, Firebase for cloud-based backend services, and a wallet-based payment system foundation to create a comprehensive user experience. The application serves three primary user groups: students who require quick and convenient meal reservations, restaurant staff who need efficient QR validation tools, and administrators who require comprehensive management and analytics capabilities.

1.3	Preliminary Study
1.3.1	Study of Existing Solutions
The market analysis reveals several categories of existing solutions for restaurant and food service management. Enterprise-level solutions such as Toast POS, Square for Restaurants, and Resy provide comprehensive restaurant management capabilities but are primarily designed for commercial establishments with different operational models and pricing structures unsuitable for university environments.

University-specific solutions include campus dining applications like GET Mobile, Grubhub for Campus, and institutional meal plan systems. However, these solutions often require significant infrastructure investments, lack integration with local payment systems, and do not address the specific constraints of Tunisian higher education institutions.

Mobile ordering platforms such as UberEats, Deliveroo, and local alternatives like Glovo Tunisia focus on delivery services rather than on-campus dining management. While these platforms demonstrate effective mobile user interfaces and payment integration, they do not provide the capacity management and access control features required for university restaurant operations.

Open-source alternatives include OpenTable for reservations and various POS systems, but these require extensive customization and technical expertise for deployment and maintenance. The analysis reveals a significant gap in the market for affordable, locally-adapted solutions that address the specific needs of Tunisian university restaurants.
 
1.3.2	Critique of Existing Solutions
The evaluation of existing solutions reveals several critical limitations that justify the development of a custom solution for ISETCOM. Commercial enterprise solutions, while feature- rich, present prohibitive licensing costs and require extensive infrastructure investments that exceed typical university budgets. These systems are designed for profit-maximizing commercial operations rather than subsidized educational services.

University-specific platforms often lack integration with local payment systems and regulatory requirements. International solutions do not support Tunisian payment gateways such as D17 and Flouci, creating barriers for local users who prefer familiar payment methods. Additionally, these platforms typically require long-term contracts and provide limited customization options for institutional-specific requirements.

Existing mobile ordering platforms focus on delivery logistics rather than on-site dining management. They lack essential features such as capacity-based reservation systems, real-time availability updates, and integration with institutional access control systems. Furthermore, commission-based pricing models are unsuitable for non-profit educational institutions.

The technical architecture of available solutions often relies on proprietary systems that limit future extensibility and integration with existing institutional systems. This creates vendor lock- in situations and reduces the institution's ability to adapt the system to evolving needs or integrate with other campus services.

2.	Market Study

2.1	Introduction

This chapter presents a structured needs analysis to ensure that the proposed mobile solution addresses real operational requirements rather than only implementing technical features. Because the ISETCOM restaurant service is used daily and experiences short but intense peak periods, the solution must satisfy three key dimensions simultaneously: (1) usability and speed for students, (2) operational efficiency for staff during high load, and (3) governance and traceability for administrators and institutional leadership. The analysis therefore identifies stakeholders, formalizes their expectations, and models typical usage scenarios to capture the real workflows that the application must support.
In addition, this chapter highlights the constraints that shape design decisions, including connectivity variability, heterogeneous mobile devices, and cost limitations. Finally, it defines the main project risks (technical and operational) and proposes mitigation strategies. The outcome of this chapter is a clear foundation for the next chapters, where requirements and design choices are specified using evidence-based needs and realistic assumptions.
2.2	Stakeholders and Roles

The system involves multiple stakeholders who interact with the service at different levels and with different objectives. At the operational level, students and restaurant staff are the most frequent users and have direct impact on daily service performance. At the governance level, administrators and institutional management define rules, monitor indicators, and ensure compliance, making them essential stakeholders for system control and sustainability.
Students represent the primary user population. Their needs are strongly time-driven: they typically access the restaurant between lectures, often within short breaks. Their priority is to reserve quickly, pay without delays, and access the restaurant without waiting. They expect the interface to be intuitive, the reservation process to be short, and confirmations to be immediate.
 
Any uncertainty (e.g., whether payment succeeded or whether the slot is still available) is perceived as a major friction point.
Restaurant staff are responsible for executing the service under real constraints: high throughput at peak hours, limited space, and a need to validate access quickly and fairly. Their main expectation is operational simplicity and speed. A scanning workflow that takes too long or produces ambiguous results directly increases pressure and slows down service. For this reason, staff tools must provide immediate feedback, detect invalid entries (expired or already-used reservations), and prevent fraud while remaining easy to use during high stress. Staff also benefit from real-time indicators (scans/hour, validation rate) that help them adapt the flow without needing manual counting.
Administrators supervise system configuration and long-term reliability. They are responsible for user management (including bulk imports), configuration of time slots and capacities, menu management, and monitoring analytics. Admin requirements are typically overlooked in small applications, but in a production system they are critical: administrators need strong access control, audit logs, and safe bulk operations to avoid large-scale errors. They also require reporting tools that transform raw activity into actionable insights for planning.
Institutional management is concerned with measurable impact, scalability, and strategic decisions. Their perspective is not limited to daily operations; they evaluate performance indicators such as adoption, waiting time reduction, food waste reduction, productivity gain, and system availability. They also assess whether the solution is stable and scalable enough to be extended to other establishments or to additional campus services.

2.3	Personas

Personas are used to represent typical users and to anchor design decisions in realistic behaviors. Instead of describing users only as "students" or "staff," personas capture motivations, constraints, and expected outcomes, which helps justify UX choices and system priorities.
Persona1- Time-constrained Student
This student attends multiple classes per day and has limited free time during lunch. Their main objective is to secure a meal without wasting time in queues. They want a fast reservation flow, clear slot availability, and a payment process that works reliably with local solutions. Any delay, uncertainty, or "pending state" that is not well explained reduces trust in the system and can lead to abandoning the digital flow and returning to manual service.

Persona2- Staff	Operator under Peak Load 
This persona represents the staff member who validates entry during high traffic. They need the scanner to be extremely reliable under real conditions (different phone models, camera quality, lighting, QR angle). Their expectations focus on speed and clarity: the system must show an unambiguous result (valid/invalid/expired/used), and feedback must be immediate to keep the flow moving. Security is critical, but it must not complicate the scanning workflow or increase cognitive load.
Persona3- Administrator(Governance and Monitoring) 
This persona configures system rules and ensures stable operation. They need tools to manage users, wallets, menus, and slots at scale. They also need traceability: actions performed in the admin interface must be logged to ensure accountability and simplify troubleshooting. Their goal is to reduce manual work and replace informal procedures with standardized digital processes supported by analytics.

2.4	User Journeys and Scenarios

User journeys describe how the system is used in real conditions and help identify critical points where failures may cause operational disruption.
Scenario 1 - Student Reservation → Payment → QR Access

The student opens the app and consults the dashboard, which provides quick access to reservation and payment actions. The student then browses available menus and selects a date and time slot. The system checks capacity constraints in real time to prevent overbooking. Once the slot is selected, the student confirms reservation details and proceeds to payment using one of the supported methods (D17, Flouci, or internal wallet). After successful payment validation, the reservation status becomes confirmed and the application generates a QR code. At the
 
restaurant entry, the student presents the QR code for scanning, which triggers server-side validation and final access confirmation.
Scenario 2 - Reservation History and Modification

After using the service multiple times, students need traceability and control. The history section allows students to verify upcoming reservations and past usage. When modification is allowed, the student selects a new date and slot, and the system updates capacity constraints accordingly. This scenario directly supports user satisfaction because it reduces frustration when schedules change. It also supports operational predictability, since updates remain recorded and consistent rather than informal.
Scenario 3 - Staff Validation Workflow

In peak time, staff must validate many users quickly. The scanning workflow must be optimized for speed and must provide immediate feedback. Each scan triggers server verification that checks reservation state (confirmed/used), time validity, and integrity rules to prevent replay and forgery. When a QR is invalid, the staff must be informed without ambiguity (e.g., expired, already used, not found). This reduces conflict, speeds up decision-making, and prevents queue blockage.
Scenario 4 - Administrative Governance

Administrators typically interact with the system to manage operational configuration. This includes importing users in bulk, setting slot templates and capacities, managing menus, and reviewing analytics dashboards. The admin workflow must be safe (validation, rollback) because mistakes at this level can affect many users. Audit logs are essential here to ensure accountability and facilitate error investigation.
2.5	Constraints

The system was designed under constraints typical of real campus environments. These constraints are not secondary; they directly influence architecture and implementation choices.
 
Connectivity variability is a major factor because students may have inconsistent network access. The application must therefore present states clearly (pending/confirmed/failed) and avoid workflows that depend on continuous connection without fallback. Reliability at the UI level is critical: users should always understand what happened and what to do next.
Device diversity also matters because students use a wide range of Android and iOS devices, including older models with limited performance. This requires careful optimization: efficient queries, controlled caching, and lightweight UI rendering. Without optimization, user experience becomes inconsistent and adoption drops.
Budget constraints require a cost-efficient backend. Using Firebase and managed services helps reduce operational overhead, but it also requires careful quota optimization (reads/writes) and efficient data modeling.
Security and privacy constraints are essential because the system processes identity data, reservation history, and payment-related workflows. This requires strong authentication, role- based access control, encryption, and careful analytics design (anonymization) to avoid exposing personal or sensitive information.
2.6	Risks and Mitigation

Because the application runs in production and directly impacts daily operations, risk management is essential. Risks were identified early and treated through design and implementation decisions.
A first major risk is high concurrency during peak hours, which can cause overbooking if multiple users reserve simultaneously. This risk is mitigated through atomic operations and controlled reservation state transitions. A second risk is payment reliability, since payment systems are sensitive to partial failures, network issues, and asynchronous confirmation. To mitigate this, the system applies retry/backoff logic, fallback between payment methods, and rollback strategies to prevent inconsistent states.
 
A third risk concerns QR security. Without strong validation, QR codes can be replayed or forged. The mitigation requires server-side validation, time-based validity rules, and integrity protections. Another risk is performance degradation on low-end devices, especially in camera- based scanning workflows; mitigation includes optimization and efficient rendering. Finally, privacy risks exist when collecting analytics; mitigations include anonymization and strict role- based access controls.
2.7	Conclusion

This chapter clarified the needs of each stakeholder group and translated them into realistic expectations through personas and operational scenarios. It demonstrated that the project is not only a mobile application, but a production service that must remain reliable under peak demand, payment constraints, and security threats. The chapter also documented practical constraints such as connectivity variability, device diversity, and cost efficiency, all of which influenced architectural decisions. Finally, the risks and mitigation strategies presented here establish a solid foundation for the next chapter, which will formalize the functional and non-functional requirements of the system in a structured and verifiable manner.

3.3	Non-Functional Requirements

Non-functional requirements define the quality attributes required for safe production operation. In practice, these requirements heavily influence architecture decisions (database design, security model, caching strategy, monitoring, and CI/CD). For this project, NFRs are aligned with production targets already observed in deployment: fast response times, high availability, strong security controls, and scalability readiness.
 
3.3.1	Performance

The system must provide fast response time for everyday operations such as authentication, dashboard loading, reservation creation, and data synchronization. Performance targets are essential because the restaurant service is time-sensitive and peak usage occurs within short windows. A slow response time directly increases user frustration and can negatively impact adoption. The application must remain responsive even on heterogeneous devices, requiring optimized queries, careful UI rendering, and efficient caching.
For staff workflows, performance is even more critical: QR validation must execute quickly to avoid blocking entry flow. Therefore, server-side validation should remain lightweight and consistently optimized.
3.3.2	Availability and Reliability

The system must achieve high availability and remain stable during daily operations. Because it is used continuously throughout the academic schedule, downtime during peak hours has direct operational consequences (queues, service disruption, staff overload). The system must therefore include reliability mechanisms such as controlled state transitions, safe retry logic, fallback paths for payment failures, and operational monitoring to detect incidents early.
In addition, the system must be designed so that failures do not lead to data corruption. For example, reservation states must remain consistent even when network connectivity is unstable.
3.3.3	Security and Privacy

Security is a core requirement because the system handles user identity, reservation records, and payment-related processes. The system must enforce secure communication (transport security), protect sensitive data (encryption), implement strong authentica tion, and restrict access via role-based authorization. QR validation must include protections against replay and forgery, because QR codes represent access rights.
 
Privacy requirements apply particularly to analytics: the system must avoid exposing personal data unnecessarily and must ensure that access to sensitive information is limited to authorized roles. Audit logs must be maintained to ensure accountability and support investigations if abnormal behavior is detected.
3.3.4	Scalability

The system must support more than 1000 concurrent users and remain ready for future expansion (multi-institution deployment). Scalability affects database design, indexing strategy, and the architecture of server-side workflows. The system must remain stable under peak loads and must be adaptable to increased traffic without requiring a complete redesign.
3.3.5	Maintainability and Quality

To ensure long-term sustainability, the project must be maintainable. This includes clean architecture (MVVM + layered design), modular components, standardized development workflow, CI/CD automation, and automated testing. Maintainability is essential because production systems evolve: menus change, policies change, payments evolve, and new campuses may join. A maintainable codebase reduces the cost and risk of future updates.

3.4.1	Acceptance Criteria (subsections)

Acceptance criteria define the objective conditions under which the system can be considered compliant with project objectives and suitable for production deployment. They transform general requirements into measurable indicators that can be validated through testing, monitoring, and real operational use. In the context of a university restaurant service that operates daily and under peak demand, acceptance criteria are essential to ensure that the digital solution is not only functional, but also reliable, secure, and operationally efficient.
The acceptance criteria are grouped into several categories reflecting the main dimensions of system quality: usability and adoption, operational efficiency, performance and responsiveness, reliability and availability, security and fraud prevention, payment consistency, administrative governance, and scalability.
3.4.2	User Adoption and Usability Criteria

The first level of acceptance concerns user adoption and usability, which are critical indicators of whether the solution effectively replaces manual processes. The system must achieve an
 
adoption rate of at least 95% of the student population, ensuring that the majority of students rely on the application instead of physical queues. This criterion is verified through usage statistics and active user monitoring.
From a usability perspective, the full reservation workflow-from opening the application to obtaining a confirmed QR code-must be achievable in a limited number of steps and without unnecessary complexity. The user interface must provide clear feedback at each stage, including reservation confirmation, payment status, and QR code availability. Notifications must be delivered reliably to remind students of upcoming reservations and to confirm successful transactions. Acceptance testing includes user observation sessions and feedback collection to ensure that students can complete tasks without external assistance.
3.4.3	Operational Efficiency and Service Throughput

A core objective of the project is to improve operational efficiency and reduce waiting time during peak periods. The QR-based validation workflow is designed to allow staff to validate entries faster than manual verification. 4.	Design and Implementation

4.1	Introduction

This chapter presents the technical design and implementation choices that enabled the transformation of the proposed solution into a stable production system. While previous chapters focused on understanding needs and defining requirements, this chapter explains how those requirements were translated into concrete software architecture, data models, security mechanisms, and operational workflows.
Design decisions in this project were driven by several constraints: the need for fast mobile performance, scalability under peak usage, strong security for payments and QR-based access, and maintainability for long-term institutional deployment. Therefore, architectural patterns, backend services, and development workflows were carefully selected to ensure robustness and extensibility.
This chapter first introduces the global system architecture, followed by the internal application architecture based on MVVM and Clean Architecture principles. It then describes the database design, security mechanisms, payment integration, AI components, and DevOps practices. Finally, it discusses performance optimization techniques and major technical challenges encountered during implementation.
4.2	Global System Architecture

The system follows a distributed client-cloud architecture in which mobile applications communicate with cloud-hosted backend services through secure APIs and real-time data channels. The architecture is designed to minimize server maintenance overhead while ensuring scalability and high availability, making it suitable for institutional environments with limited infrastructure resources.
 
At the client level, the system consists of three Flutter-based mobile interfaces: student app, staff app, and admin interface, which share the same codebase but expose different modules based on authenticated roles. This approach reduces development effort while maintaining strict access separation. The applications interact with Firebase services for authentication, real -time database access, file storage, analytics, and cloud functions.
At the backend level, Firebase Authentication manages identity and role assignment using custom claims, ensuring that each user receives only the permissions associated with their role. Cloud Firestore acts as the main data store, providing scalable NoSQL storage with real-time synchronization. Firebase services implement server-side business logic such as reservation validation, capacity enforcement, QR verification, and payment processing.
External services are integrated into this architecture through secure APIs. Payment providers (D17 and Flouci) communicate with backend services using encrypted channels and asynchronous callbacks. Monitoring and observability are ensured using Firebase Analytics, Crashlytics, and Performance Monitoring, enabling proactive detection of anomalies and performance degradation.

4.2	Application Architecture (MVVM and Clean Architecture)

To ensure long-term maintainability and scalability, the mobile application follows MVVM (Model-View-ViewModel) combined with Clean Architecture principles. This structure enforces separation of concerns and prevents business logic from being tightly coupled to UI components.
The Presentation Layer contains Flutter widgets responsible only for rendering UI and handling user interactions. These widgets do not access databases or APIs directly. Instead, they communicate with ViewModels that expose observable state and trigger actions. This separation improves testability and reduces UI complexity.
The ViewModel Layer acts as the mediator between UI and business logic. ViewModels manage application state, validation rules, and error handling. They call use cases from the domain layer and expose results through reactive streams or state notifiers, ensuring that UI updates remain consistent and predictable.
The Domain Layer contains business rules expressed as use cases (e.g., create reservation, confirm payment, validate QR). This layer is independent of external frameworks, allowing logic to remain stable even if backend technologies change. Domain entities define core models such as User, Reservation, TimeSlot, and PaymentTransaction.
The Data Layer handles all interactions with external systems, including Firestore queries, Cloud Function calls, and payment APIs. Repositories abstract data sources, ensuring that the domain layer does not depend on Firebase-specific implementations. This design allows mocking during tests and supports future backend evolution.
This layered approach significantly improved debugging, testing, and onboarding of new contributors. It also allowed parallel development of UI and backend integration while maintaining stable interfaces between layers.
 
4.3	Database Design and Firestore Data Modeling

The database design is based on Firestore's document-oriented NoSQL model, optimized for real- time synchronization and horizontal scalability. Collections are structured around functional domains rather than relational joins, minimizing cross-collection dependencies and improving performance.
The users collection stores authentication-linked profiles and role metadata. Each document includes user identifiers, role claims (student, staff, admin), wallet balances, and preference settings. Sensitive attributes are protected through Firestore security rules and are only accessible to authorized roles.
The reservations collection is the transactional core of the system. Each reservation document includes references to user ID, selected time slot, payment status, QR signature, and lifecycle state (pending, confirmed, used, cancelled). Indexes are configured to optimize queries by date, slot, and user ID, supporting both operational dashboards and analytics queries.
The time_slots collection defines capacity and scheduling rules. Slots are generated using templates and can be adjusted dynamically based on historical data analysis. Atomic updates are used when modifying slot capacity to prevent race conditions during high-concurrency reservation attempts.
The daily_menus collection stores meal data, nutritional information, allergen tags, and pricing. This allows menu planning to be data-driven and integrated with simple recommendation algorithms.
The analytics_records collection stores anonymized events related to user behavior, reservation flow performance, and validation metrics. This dataset feeds standard reporting dashboards is
Firestore transactions and batch writes are used extensively to maintain data consistency, especially during payment confirmation and QR validation workflows. Composite indexes are carefully designed to balance performance and quota usage.

4.4	Security Architecture and Access Control

Security is a fundamental design concern because the system manages financial transactions and access authorization. Multiple layers of protection are implemented to prevent unauthorized access, fraud, and data leakage.
All communication between client applications and backend services is protected using TLS encryption, preventing interception and tampering. Sensitive data stored in the backend is encrypted using industry-standard algorithms. Authentication is handled through Firebase
 
Authentication,  and  authorization  is enforced  using  role-based access control (RBAC)
implemented via custom claims and Firestore security rules.

Each API call is validated against the user's role and privileges. Students cannot access staff scanning APIs, and staff cannot access administrative configuration endpoints. Administrative actions are logged for audit purposes, ensuring accountability.
QR code security relies on server-side validation. QR codes include signed data and timestamps, preventing reuse and manipulation. During validation, the backend checks reservation state, timestamp validity, and cryptographic integrity before granting access. Replay attacks are prevented by marking reservations as used immediately after validation.
Payment workflows are protected through secure API calls and webhook verification. Only validated callbacks from trusted payment providers can update reservation states. Any mismatch between payment and reservation status triggers rollback mechanisms to avoid unauthorized access.


4.5	Payment Integration and Transaction Reliability

Payment integration is a critical component of system trust and financial accountability. The application supports multiple payment methods to maximize accessibility and reliability, including national banking services and mobile wallets.
 
Payment processing follows a multi-step confirmation workflow. After a user initiates payment, the backend generates a transaction reference and redirects to the provider's payment interface. Payment confirmation is received asynchronously via webhooks, which are validated using secure signatures before updating reservation states.
Fallback mechanisms are implemented to handle partial failures. If one provider is unavailable or fails, the user can retry using another method without losing the reservation context. Transactions are reconciled periodically to ensure consistency between financial records and reservation confirmations.
All financial operations are logged and linked to reservation records, supporting audit and compliance requirements. These mechanisms ensure that no confirmed reservation exists without verified payment.
4.6	Technical Challenges and Solutions

Several technical challenges were encountered during implementation. High concurrency during peak reservation periods required careful transaction handling to avoid overbooking. This was solved using atomic Firestore operations and capacity locks.
Performance optimization was required for older devices, particularly during QR scanning. Lazy loading, optimized image processing, and efficient state management significantly improved responsiveness.
Security concerns around QR replay and payment fraud required server-side enforcement and cryptographic validation. Multiple layers of validation were implemented to eliminate attack vectors.
These challenges significantly shaped architectural decisions and strengthened system robustness.
